
=== market-and-comparison-engine
HEADLINE: The "paste a link, compare prices" part of the idea is not a moat in 2026. Several Indian players already do it: 91mobiles/Smartprix/PriceHistory/Buyhatke, and Buyhatke's extension alone has about 1M users. The ones with public numbers are small: 91mobiles made Rs 49.8 Cr revenue in FY25, and Buyhatke lost its Amazon affiliate ID. Google also started testing Flipkart checkout inside Gemini and AI Mode on 26 Sep 2026. Doing it at scale is harder than it looks, for three reasons. (1) Getting Amazon and Flipkart data legally is gated: PA-API was retired in May 2026, the Creators API needs referred sales, Flipkart has closed affiliate signups, the terms ban scraping, and OLX v Padawan is a precedent against it. (2) Offline store prices are in no data feed anywhere. (3) Brands deliberately split models by channel. Samsung sells near-identical online-only models such as DUE76 on Flipkart vs DUE77 on Amazon, and its 2025 UE81/84/86 range is "targeted specifically for e-commerce", so exact matching of "the same TV" often fails. Most of the real price difference now sits in bank offers, exchange and EMI rather than the list price. In one live Flipkart listing for a Rs 26,990 Samsung TV, the bank offer was about 5% and exchange up to about 15% of the price. Flipkart expects about a third of its electronics GMV to go through EMI in the 2026 festive season. The pool is the only part that could be different, but group-buying has a poor record for durables. Mercata and MobShop failed. Groupon India ended as Nearbuy and a distress sale. CityMall's founders said group buying is hard in India because "no two people will ever ask for the same product at the same time", and Temu dropped Pinduoduo's team-buy feature outside China. The pooled-buying models that do work in India are closed, verified groups, such as Samsung Corporate+, which over 3 lakh employees have used, and gated-society deals. So the concept holds up only if it becomes a dealer quote marketplace for closed local groups, grouped by kind of product rather than exact SKU. A generic all-India scan-and-pool app would not work.

- [high] Getting 'all platforms in seconds' legally is hard: official access is gated and scraping is barred by the platforms' terms :: Amazon PA-API 5.0 stopped taking new users, was deprecated on 30 Apr 2026 and retired on 15 May 2026. Its successor, the Creators API, is open only to Associates who have 'referred qualified sales' and passed final review; third-party reports say this means 10 sales in 30 days, a cold-start problem. Amazon's terms limit cached prices to 1 hour, require a timestamp and disclaimer on every displayed price, and ban training models on Amazon data. The Amazon Conditions of Use ban data mining and robots. Flipkart's affiliate programme has been closed to new public signups since around 2023, and its product and offer APIs serve only registered affiliates. Under Indian law, IT Act s.43 penalises extracting data without permission, contract breach of terms of use is actionable, and in OLX v Padawan (2016) the Delhi High Court restrained scraping that republished a database. - [medium] No data source exists for offline store prices; the only way to get them is to ask dealers :: Croma, Reliance Digital and Vijay Sales publish online prices through affiliate networks, and those prices depend on the pincode. Local dealers and brand-authorised stores publish nothing. ONDC does not help here: electronics 'has failed to attract serious large-brand participation.' Offline quotes cannot be part of the few-seconds scan; they come later, as quotes over hours. - [high] Group-buying of durable goods has failed repeatedly, for structural reasons :: Mercata (Paul Allen-backed, about $89M raised) shut in Jan 2001, and MobShop dropped its consumer business because 'running costs were too high and consumer set too diverse'. Buyers had to wait, and big retailers were often cheaper anyway because they buy far larger volumes than any pool. Groupon India became Nearbuy in 2015 and was sold to Paytm in a distress deal for Rs 272 Cr after the coupon industry stalled. Pinduoduo's team-buy works for low-price, impulse items with 24-hour windows and social sharing. Even Temu dropped 'Team Up, Price Down' outside China, partly because users were flooded with referral codes. In the online sales that matter here, Amazon and Flipkart already pool the whole country's demand and get brand-funded festive pricing. A 50-unit pool is tiny next to that. - [medium] LLM spec extraction works well enough to fill in specs, but not to decide that two listings are the same product :: On the MAVE attribute-extraction benchmark, GPT-4o-mini and Gemini 2.5 Flash score F1 of 0.496 and 0.513 on exact match, and 0.617 and 0.622 on fuzzy match. 23.2% of exact-match 'failures' were actually correct but formatted differently, e.g. 'Queen' vs 'Queen size' (arXiv 2607.22949, 2026). RISKS: Google (Gemini/AI Mode with Flipkart checkout), ChatGPT (NPCI UPI agentic pilot), Amazon Rufus and the existing comparison apps all offer free comparison, so the comparison feature cannot justify raising money or charging. | Amazon or Flipkart can cut off data access or affiliate IDs at any time (Buyhatke's precedent). Scraping at scale risks IT Act s.43 claims, contract breach and an OLX v Padawan-style injunction; the Amazon Creators API needs referred sales before you can use it. | Wrongly calling two SKUs 'the same' (e.g. DUE76 vs DUE77, online vs offline models) can mislead buyers and draw complaints to the CCPA (consumer regulator) and returns disputes. Claims like 'cheapest' or 'saves Rs X' that ignore user-specific bank and exchange offers carry the same risk. | Pools may never fill: demand is spread across hundreds of SKUs and channel-specific model codes, so a 50-unit pool for one exact SKU in one city within a short window is unlikely outside closed groups. | Offline authorised dealers may not quote below MOP, or may not stock the online-only SKU; brands may quietly discourage dealers from joining visible price-cutting platforms. | Timing: Oct-Nov festive pricing plus 10% bank offers (Axis/ICICI on Flipkart BBD 2026) will usually beat any 20-50 unit dealer quote, so pilots run in the festive season will look like failures. | 2026 memory-chip-driven price rises (TVs up 7-10%) squeeze dealer margins and shift discounts into financing, which a pool platform does not control.
RECS: Treat comparison as a free way to attract users, not the product. Do not build an all-India scraper. Use official channels (Amazon Associates and later the Creators API once you have referred sales; Croma, Reliance Digital and Tata Cliq via Cuelinks, EarnKaro or INRDeals feeds). When a user pastes a link, fetch that page on the user's device or from a user-initiated request, and show a timestamp on every price. | Start narrow: one city (Hyderabad), one or two categories (ACs before summer, Feb-Apr; 55-inch+ TVs) and a hand-checked catalogue of the top 100-150 SKUs. Map each online-only SKU to its nearest offline equivalent by hand and show the differences. | Pool on a kind of product rather than an exact model code: brand + size + panel type + year (e.g. 'Samsung 55-inch 4K Crystal 2026'). Let each dealer quote their own SKU with a spec comparison. This reduces demand fragmentation and lets offline dealers take part. | Show the effective price per user, not the list price: ask which cards they hold, whether they have an old TV/AC to exchange, their pincode, and whether they need installation or stand/copper extras. Compare pool quotes against that number. Without this, the 'pool is cheaper' claim will often be false. | Run a manual MVP before writing matching code: WhatsApp plus a form, 5-10 brand-authorised dealers in Hyderabad, and a weekly quote round for 10-30 units. Measure (a) how often dealers quote below the online effective price, split by off-season vs festive, and (b) pool fill rate. Kill or pivot if dealers beat online effective price in less than about 30% of rounds. | Seed pools with closed, verified groups (company employee groups by work email, like Samsung Corporate+; gated societies via RWA or MyGate-style partnerships). This gives dense local demand for dealer delivery, verified identities against fake accounts, and social pressure against mass cancellations. | Monetise from the supply side: a dealer success fee or lead fee (e.g. 1-3%, paid by the dealer), rather than an Amazon affiliate cut of 3.5% on TVs that can be revoked, or a margin hidden inside the pool price. | Do not pilot in the Oct-Nov festive window. Run the first pools in Jan-Mar (ACs, and TVs after the sales) when online discounts are thinner and dealers want volume.
OPEN: Will brand-authorised dealers in Hyderabad actually quote below MOP for 10-50 unit pools, and will the brands (Samsung, LG, Sony, Voltas, Daikin) tolerate a public platform showing those prices? | What share of pasted Amazon or Flipkart links point to online-only SKUs that no offline dealer can supply? Measure it on the top 150 TV and AC SKUs. | Outside the festive season, what is the typical gap between the online effective price (after bank offer, exchange and installation extras) and the best offline dealer quote for the same product type? | Can the platform meet Amazon Creators API eligibility (referred sales, reportedly about 10 in 30 days) early, and would Amazon object to showing its price next to a competing pool price, as it did with Buyhatke? | Did ShopMate (Delhi, 2015), which ran almost this exact model for phones, laptops and two-wheelers, shut down, and why? Its founders are worth interviewing. | Would Samsung Corporate+ or similar brand programmes see a pooling platform as a partner that brings in employee groups, or as channel conflict?

=== supply-and-fulfilment
HEADLINE: You can stay a pure mediator, but only if the authorised dealer is the seller of record. The dealer issues a GST invoice in each buyer's name and delivers, either with its own staff or through a courier it pays for (Porter, Shadowfax, Delhivery). The brand handles installation, confirms dead-on-arrival (DOA) units and honours the warranty; brand installation already works this way whichever dealer sold the TV. The platform owns the customer relationship, the money, service deadlines and grievances. Amazon and Flipkart already work this way for TVs. They take no change-of-mind returns: the remedy is a 7-10 day replacement or repair through the brand's service centre, often backed by a brand DOA certificate. So a pool can copy this model without building a delivery network. What makes it work is controlling the money: under RBI's 2025 rules for payment aggregators, you and your payment provider agree how long before dealers are paid. That lets you hold the dealer's money until delivery is confirmed and the DOA window closes. Three facts from the research constrain the idea. (1) Dealer economics are thin. Listed electronics retailers make about 14-16% gross and about 7% EBITDA (Electronics Mart FY25: 14.6% / 6.9%), and TV margins are lower still. A local dealer can fund only about 2-5% off the brand's minimum selling price (MOP) on a Rs 30k TV. Bigger pool discounts need money from the brand, and brands quietly punish dealers who sell below MOP, even though India's competition regulator (CCI) fined Maruti Rs 200 crore in 2021 for exactly that kind of discount control. (2) Speed: Flipkart promises next-day delivery plus installation within hours in its top 13 cities, and Amazon offers scheduled delivery with installation in 220+ cities. A pool cannot match that; it will take 2-5 days (the pool window plus dealer delivery). (3) Dealers serve one region and TVs are bulky: shipping a 55-inch TV to another city probably costs Rs 1,300-2,600 in freight before any breakage risk (my estimate). So pools should be city-level, starting with Hyderabad, not pan-India. The Open Network for Digital Commerce (ONDC) gives a ready-made template for splitting responsibility between buyer app, seller app and delivery partner, but it is not a supply source today. Its retail orders fell from 6.5M in Oct 2024 to about 4.3-4.6M in early 2025, big electronics brands have not joined, and PhonePe's Pincode app stopped selling to consumers in Dec 2025. Finally, even as a 'mediator' you can be held jointly liable: a Mumbai consumer commission rejected Flipkart's 'mere intermediary' defence over a faulty TV in June 2025.
- [medium] Consumer-electronics distribution runs brand -> (C&F/super-stockist) -> distributor (~5-8%) -> dealer (~8-15% on TVs/electronics, 10-15% on large appliances). Total channel cost is 20-35% of MRP, and listed retailers confirm thin economics. :: Advisory benchmarks: TV/electronics distributor 5-8%, general-trade dealer 8-15%, modern trade 8-12% plus listing fees; large appliances 10-15% dealer. Worked example: a Rs 30,000-MRP fridge sells to the consumer for Rs 27,000-28,500; the dealer buys at Rs 23,000-25,000. Dealer credit terms of 15-30 days add 1-2% to cost. Listed retailers: Electronics Mart India FY25 gross margin 14.6% (down 101 bps), EBITDA 6.9%. Aditya Vision gross margin ~15.6% (Q3 FY25). JM Financial's FY23 peer set shows gross margins of roughly 8-17%, with Croma the lowest EBITDA peer, apparently negative (~-2.5%), in that chart. Mobiles and TVs earn less than large appliances. My estimate for a Rs 30k TV: after delivery (Rs 300-700 locally), card payment fees (~2%) and a platform fee, a dealer can fund only about 2-5% off MOP from its own margin. Deeper pool discounts need brand money (sell-out or volume-slab support). === legal-tax-payments
HEADLINE: The idea is legally workable in India, but only in one shape: a pure marketplace. The dealer is the seller of record, issues the GST invoice and delivers. The platform earns a disclosed commission or fee and never marks up the dealer's invoice. Buyer money moves only through an RBI-authorised payment aggregator's split/escrow product, never through the startup's own bank account. Being a "mediator" does not make you liability-free. The E-Commerce Rules 2020 (tightened by the 2026 Amendment, effective 1 Jan 2027) put grievance, disclosure, ranking, pricing and dark-pattern duties on you. Consumer commissions routinely hold marketplaces jointly liable with the seller, for example Flipkart plus Thomson TV in June 2025. So delivery and returns must be handled by contract: dealer SLAs, settlement held until delivery, seller indemnity. For "commit now, final price later, auto-refund if the pool fails", the best rail is UPI One-Time Mandate: block up to about Rs 1 lakh for up to 60 days, capture a lower final amount once, and the rest is released automatically. UPI Reserve Pay (SBMD) does not work: NPCI caps it at Rs 10,000 and limits it to low-ticket, high-frequency merchants. Card pre-auths lapse in about 3-7 days. Both abuse scenarios in Q3 are best stopped by payment design rather than ID checks. The price tier should depend on units actually captured at close, not on sign-ups. Any cancellation fee after the lock is lawful only if it is symmetric (Rule 4(8)) and proportionate (CPA s.2(46)). Tax is manageable: 0.5% GST TCS with mandatory ECO registration, 0.1% income-tax TDS (s.194-O, now s.393(1)), and 18% GST on your commission. The two real regulatory hazards are (a) the "margin" (it turns you into a reseller if you take title, which is fatal if you ever take foreign money) and (b) scraping retailer sites instead of using affiliate APIs.
- [high] Being 'only a mediator' still makes you a regulated 'marketplace e-commerce entity' under the Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules 2020, and the 2026 Amendment (notified 9 Sep 2026, in force 1 Jan 2027) adds more duties. :: Rule 3 defines a marketplace entity as one that provides an IT platform 'to facilitate transactions between buyers and sellers', which is exactly this model. Duties that already apply under Rule 4 and Rule 5:
- Display your legal name, address and contact details.
- Appoint a grievance officer who acknowledges complaints within 48 hours and resolves them within 1 month.
- Join the National Consumer Helpline.
- Get explicit consent (no pre-ticked boxes).
- Refund within a reasonable time per RBI norms.
- Do not manipulate prices.
- Show each seller's name, address, customer care number and ratings, plus return, refund, warranty and delivery information.
- Explain the main parameters that decide ranking.
- Keep the IT Act s.79 intermediary safe-harbour conditions (Rule 5(1)).
The 2026 Amendment adds:
- Any 'was' price must be the lowest price in the preceding 30 days.
- Ranking factors must be disclosed in descending order of importance.
- Sponsored listings must be labelled.
- Annual dark-pattern self-audit, with a compliance certificate shown on the platform.
- Seller name on the invoice in the same font size as the platform's name.
- Seller details must be given on request.
- Separate express consent before using consumer data for certain purposes (for example promoting the platform's own-brand goods). - [high] Cancellation charges on buyers are allowed only if the platform bears similar charges when it (or the seller) cancels, and they must not be disproportionate. This sets the legal limits of your anti-abuse penalty for Q3(b). :: Rule 4(8): 'No e-commerce entity shall impose cancellation charges on consumers cancelling after confirming purchase unless similar charges are also borne by the e-commerce entity, if they cancel the purchase order unilaterally for any reason.'

CPA 2019 s.2(46) separately treats these contract terms as 'unfair':
- 'manifestly excessive security deposits'
- a penalty 'wholly disproportionate to the loss' caused by the breach
Unfair-contract complaints go to State and National Commissions.

What this means for the pool:
- A fixed post-lock cancellation fee (for example Rs 300-500, or 1-2% of the unit price) is defensible if the same amount is paid to the buyer when the dealer or platform cancels, and if it roughly reflects the real loss (a re-priced tier or the dealer's cost).
- Forfeiting a large deposit is risky.
- India has no statutory change-of-mind cooling-off right for online goods. Sellers must accept returns only for defective, not-as-described or late goods, and the return policy must be disclosed up front. (https://www.scconline.com/blog/post/2020/07/24/consumer-protection-e-commerce-rules-2020-comes-into-force-no-cancellation-charges-on-consumers-cancelling-after-confirming-purchase-read-notification/ ; https://indiankanoon.org/doc/62183455/)
- [high] Q2 (delivery, returns): in the marketplace model the seller legally owns delivery, warranty and customer satisfaction, but consumer commissions still hold platforms jointly liable. 'Mediator' status reduces your exposure; it does not remove it. :: The FDI e-commerce policy (PIB, Dec 2018) states: 'Post sales, delivery of goods to the customers and customer satisfaction will be responsibility of the seller', and warranties and guarantees are the seller's responsibility. It also allows marketplaces to provide logistics, fulfilment, call-centre and payment-collection support.

In practice, commissions often look past this:
- South Mumbai District Commission (June 2025): Flipkart and Thomson TV held 'jointly and severally liable' for a defective TV. Order: refund Rs 13,999 plus interest, Rs 15,000 for mental agony and Rs 5,000 costs. Flipkart's 'mere intermediary' defence was rejected.
- North-East Delhi Commission: similar order against Flipkart and Lenovo.

The 2021 draft 'fall-back liability' clause was not included in the 2026 Amendment. Liability therefore comes from case law, not a rule, but it is real.

What this means operationally:
- The dealer is the seller of record and ships directly to each buyer (use their own logistics or a shipping aggregator booked by the dealer). Do not hold stock at a hub; that also avoids an 'inventory control' finding.
- Installation goes through the brand's service network.
- The platform needs contractual SLAs, doorstep-OTP delivery confirmation, a settlement holdback and seller indemnities so it can refund first and recover from the dealer later. - [high] FDI rules (Press Note 2 of 2018): a foreign-funded marketplace must not 'directly or indirectly influence the sale price' and cannot own inventory for Indian consumers. Press Note 3 of 2026 opened inventory ownership only for exports. An Indian-owned startup is not bound yet, but a platform-set 'margin' is structurally risky. :: Press Note 2 (2018) says marketplace entities 'shall not directly or indirectly influence the sale price of goods and services and maintain a level playing field'. It also bars exclusivity mandates and bars selling through vendors in which the marketplace holds equity or controls inventory.

Press Note 3 (2026), announced 23 Jul 2026 and operationalised 5 Aug 2026 (DGFT Notification 27/2026-27), allows foreign-funded entities to hold inventory only to export Indian-made goods. Foreign-funded inventory-based sales to Indian consumers remain prohibited.

These conditions bind only entities with foreign investment. An Indian-owned-and-controlled company could legally buy from the dealer and resell at a markup. Doing so makes it an 'inventory e-commerce entity' (Rule 7 duties), the GST supplier and the likely product seller for product-liability purposes, and forces a restructure the day a foreign VC invests.

How the price should work:
- A reverse auction where each dealer bids its own all-in consumer price is seller-set pricing, which is fine.
- A platform-decided markup on top of the winning bid is 'influencing sale price'.
- Cleanest option: the dealer's bid is the consumer price, and the platform takes a seller-paid commission at settlement.
- Alternatively, charge a separately disclosed and invoiced buyer 'platform fee', as food-delivery apps do. GST on the goods:

=== abuse-and-mechanism-design
HEADLINE: Both of your fears are real, but they share one design flaw you can remove. Fake accounts (A) and "commit then 49 cancel" (B) only work if the lower price is based on a count that includes people who never pay or never keep the product. The fix is to set the price by units actually paid for and delivered. Each buyer blocks or pays up to a guaranteed ceiling (today's best available price) through a UPI one-time mandate, a short card pre-authorisation, or payment into a payment-aggregator escrow. Dealers submit sealed price curves (price at 10, 25 and 50 units). The platform charges a cautious amount at close. The final discount is refunded only after delivery and the replacement window, based on how many units were actually delivered. Under this design a fake account cannot lower anyone's price without buying a Rs 30k TV from its own KYC'd bank account, and 49 cancellations just move the price back up the curve. You then don't need cancellation penalties, which is good: Rule 4(8) of India's E-Commerce Rules 2020 bars charging buyers for cancelling unless the platform bears similar charges when it cancels. If one organiser brings 50 real buyers who each pay from their own account, take delivery at their own address and keep the product, that is not abuse. It is the product working, and it deserves a referral reward. The line is crossed only when a benefit is taken without a completed purchase (A, B), when units pile up with one person for resale (C), or when fake buyers are used to farm incentives (D). The bigger threats you haven't considered are on the dealer and brand side: brand price floors for dealers (the "market operating price") can make them afraid to bid; dealers can collude, bid low and then fail to deliver, or ship grey or display units; buyers and dealers can use your pool to discover the price and then deal directly; and fake "pool size" counters would be a dark-pattern problem with the consumer regulator (CCPA).
- [high] Attacks A and B are made impossible by where the price is set, not by stronger identity checks: tie the discount to units that are paid and delivered. :: Mechanism: (1) When the pool is created, the ceiling is set to the best landed price found across platforms. (2) Each joiner blocks or pays up to that ceiling. (3) The pool closes and dealers submit sealed price curves. (4) At close the platform charges a cautious price, e.g. the curve price one tier below the committed count, and unblocks the rest. (5) Buyers get a free 24-hour cooling-off window, then the order is dispatched. (6) Delivery is confirmed by OTP plus the TV's serial number. (7) After the replacement window (about 7-10 days) the platform recalculates the price from the delivered count and refunds the difference. Attack A (49 fake accounts) now needs 49 distinct KYC'd funding sources, each blocking Rs 30k (Rs 14.7 lakh in total), and it still earns nothing unless all 49 TVs are actually bought and kept. That is no longer fraud; it becomes the reseller case (C). Attack B (49 cancel or refuse delivery) just drops the delivered count to 1, so the attacker's unit is recalculated at the 1-unit price (the ceiling) and gets no discount. A deposit-only version is also safe on price, because the price is still set by delivered units. The size of the commitment only affects how much dealers can trust the numbers and how cheap it is to disrupt a pool. (Mechanism analysis. Precedents: Groupon tipping point (https://patents.justia.com/patent/8301495), Kickstarter all-or-nothing (https://help.kickstarter.com/hc/en-us/articles/115005047893-Why-is-funding-all-or-nothing), Pinduoduo team purchase (https://www.cnbc.com/2020/04/22/what-is-pinduoduo-chinese-ecommerce-rival-to-alibaba.html))
- [high] Penalising cancellations after the pool closes (forfeiting deposits) is legally fragile in India. The recalculation design avoids needing it. :: Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules 2020, Rule 4(8): 'No e-commerce entity shall impose cancellation charges on consumers cancelling after confirming purchase unless similar charges are also borne by the e-commerce entity, if they cancel the purchase order unilaterally for any reason.' A symmetric penalty is possible in principle (platform or dealer pays the buyer the same amount if it cancels), but it is consumer-hostile and needs a legal opinion. Other rules that still apply to you as a pure intermediary: Rule 4(5) (grievance officer acknowledges within 48 hours, resolves within 1 month); Rule 4(10) (refunds within a reasonable time); Rule 4(11)(a) (no 'unjustified price' given market conditions); Rule 5 (show seller details and a full price breakup of compulsory and voluntary charges); Rule 6 (seller cannot refuse return or refund for defective, spurious or late goods). Denying future pool access to users who repeatedly walk away is a service restriction, not a charge, and is the safer lever. - [high] Dealer collusion is a legal risk as well as a fraud risk: under Indian competition law, bid rigging is presumed harmful to competition. :: Section 3(3)(d) of the Competition Act presumes that 'bid rigging or collusive bidding' has an appreciable adverse effect on competition, and the parties carry the burden of proving otherwise. - [medium] Bypassing the platform (E) and competitor price-probing (F) are information leaks, not fraud. They are controlled by what you reveal and to whom. :: A sealed winning price is valuable.
